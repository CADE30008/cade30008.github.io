AI in this course¶

This page sets out how I think about generative AI in engineering and in learning; my advice for students; how I use it to make and maintain these materials; and how you can help improve them.

How I think about AI¶

AI rewards expertise¶

Sean Goedecke makes an argument I agree with: language models reward expertise.1 They let anyone produce seemingly passable work in almost any field, but many outputs fall down against an expert eye, and (importantly in engineering) against the real world.

Goedecke's example is Fields Medal-winning mathematician Terence Tao's conversation with ChatGPT about the Jacobian conjecture, on 20 July 2026.2 Tao's skill is evident, and demonstrates advanced LLM usage, but its utility is highly dependent on human judgement in prompting and evaluating output.

For you, the engineering student, understanding the fundamentals of the discipline is what will make you a good engineer, and a responsible and effective user of whatever tools support you in your work. If you don't, you'll just be wrong faster, and won't know.

Engineering is a real-world discipline and the quality and diligence of our work can have life-or-death impact. Accountability lies with the human-in-the-loop.

Work or gym?¶

In September 2026, Tao and 24 other Fields Medallists published a joint declaration on the impact of AI on mathematics, the people within it, and the future of the discipline.3 Part of their argument is about how mathematicians are made. Considering this is important in how we support and assess our students.

Bruce Schneier uses an analogy.4 Before using AI on a task, ask whether the task is work or gym.

  • Work is a task where only the outcome matters. Nobody cares how it got done, as long as it's done well, noting that - particularly in engineering - accountability matters.
  • Gym is a task where doing it is the point, because doing it is what builds your ability. Sending a machine to lift your weights moves the weights, but you don't change.

At university, most tasks are gym, even when they look like work. Writing up a design feels like work, but the thinking you do to write it clearly is the gym, and it's what we're trying to assess.

The same task can be either, depending on why you're doing it. Chasing down an obscure Simulink error at eleven at night, when you already understand what your model should do, is (mostly) work. Working out why your loop has less phase margin than you expected is the gym: the struggle is what changes you.

What that means in this unit¶

  • You may use AI, including the AI and agentic tools in MATLAB and Simulink, in the ways the coursework brief lists. The coursework is in the University's Category 3, "Selective".
  • You won't need it. You can do everything in this unit, to the highest standard, without any AI tool.
  • You are responsible for everything you submit, including anything a tool produces. Checking what an AI tool gives you is now an engineering skill.
  • If you can't explain it, you don't understand it yet.

How these materials were made¶

I use generative AI tools to help create these resources: drafting outlines, writing and checking code, building this site, and keeping all of the pieces consistent with each other.

The pedagogy and the content are mine: what is taught, in what order, how, and why. I have quality-assured and rewritten all of the content.

Teaching, and all formative and summative assessment, are carried out by people. Automated scripts help with technical checks, such as whether your code runs and reproduces your results; those scripts are conventional code, not generative AI, and they don't award marks.

Why I work this way¶

A course like this is a complex learning journey. A series of lectures, with handouts, slide decks, example sheets, code in two languages, figures, and interactives; a coursework that builds through the term; periodic checks; a hands-on laboratory. Every piece has to agree with every other, and has to keep agreeing, every year, as I change things.

That is a great deal of consistency to hold in one head, and it is exactly the kind of tracking a language model is good at — provided it works to robust, explicit, written guidance, and a subject and teaching expert stays in the loop.

I have a suite of 'principles' and 'implementation' docs that I use behind the scenes to try to keep the LLM doing things in the way that I want, but it's often a time-consuming battle.

I use scripts that I've developed with LLM support to check that numbers, figures, slides and handouts agree: every number on a slide traces to the handout, every figure to the code that made it, and every code snippet has been run, in Python and MATLAB, with both giving the same answer. The model tracks; I decide. I use a back-end tool that I and an LLM have developed to keep track of review, approval, and release of materials.

I'm new to this. I'm using this course to develop principles that I want to deploy in other courses, and to share with other educators — as long as it supports my students' learning and experience.

Where I've gone wrong so far¶

It's easy to begin to allow the LLM to think for you - it can provide suggestions, links to learning theory, draft content, all the way to producing the full unit itself. It's especially easy to start to lean on these tools when things get busy and time is short.

The first few lectures I delivered in this way were in a busy time and I went too far, embarrassed myself, and didn't do my students justice - I'm grateful to those of them who gave constructive feedback that got me back on track.

My strong recommendation is that it's far better to do fewer things well than try to do everything you can possibly dream of but skimp on the QA.

Tell me what you think¶

I welcome feedback, both specific and general: an error in a derivation, a figure that doesn't make sense, a badly posed question, a broken link — or a view on whether this whole approach works.

  • On GitHub. If you're comfortable doing so, raise an issue in the course's repository. An issue is simply a public note describing a problem; you don't need to know how to use git. Say which page, and what's wrong.
  • By email. You're equally welcome to write to me at steve.bullock@bristol.ac.uk.
